# Recording script: read this into your phone

**How to record (5 minutes):**

1. Find a quiet room with soft things around (a bedroom with curtains is better than a bathroom or a hall). Turn off fans and AC.
2. Use your phone's voice recorder app. Hold the phone about a hand-span (15–20 cm) from your mouth, slightly to the side.
3. Read **every numbered line, in order, once**. After each line, **pause for about 2 seconds** of silence. That pause is how the video finds each line, so don't skip it.
4. Don't pause for long *inside* a line (a normal comma breath is fine).
5. Made a mistake? Stop, wait 3 seconds, and start the **whole recording again**. One clean take, start to finish, is what we need.
6. Lines 1–16: calm and serious. From line 17 on: smile while you talk, more energy, it's the hopeful part.
7. Send the file as it is (m4a, mp3, wav: any format works). See the README for how to get it to me.

Say "Korle Bu" and "KNUST" the way you normally do. Numbers can be read naturally ("twenty twenty-six", "one hundred and eighteen").

## Opening

**1.** In Ghana, there is a phrase every emergency worker knows.

**2.** No bed syndrome.

## The story (calm, serious)

**3.** On the 6th of February 2026, Charles Amissah, a 29-year-old engineer, was hit by a car at the Circle overpass in Accra.

**4.** Paramedics reached him and stabilised him.

**5.** Then the ambulance went to the Police Hospital.

**6.** Then to Ridge Hospital.

**7.** Then to Korle Bu.

**8.** Each time, the answer was the same: no bed.

**9.** A Ministry of Health committee later described a 118-minute referral loop. He did not survive.

## The numbers

**10.** His story is not rare.

**11.** Ghana has fewer than one hospital bed for every 1,000 people: about 0.7 to 0.9.

**12.** Major hospitals report running at 120 to 150 percent of their capacity.

**13.** And in mid-2025, Greater Accra, a region of over five million people, had only about 42 ambulances available.

## The real problem

**14.** But beds are only part of the problem. The deeper failure is information.

**15.** When an ambulance leaves the scene, nobody knows which hospital has a free bed.

**16.** Crews find out by driving there. Every wrong stop costs minutes a patient may not have.

## BedAlert: energy UP from here 🔥

**17.** So we are building BedAlert: one live view of every free hospital bed, so nobody has to search blind ever again!

## For hospitals

**18.** Hospitals update their free beds in one tap, or connect their own systems so the numbers update automatically.

**19.** And when a patient is on the way, the ward gets an alert, and accepts or declines with a single tap.

**20.** The bed is ready before the ambulance even arrives.

## For dispatchers

**21.** Ambulance dispatchers see the nearest hospital with a confirmed free bed, and lock it in before the ambulance even moves.

**22.** And if a hospital declines? BedAlert instantly finds the next one.

## For families

**23.** Families can find a free bed near them, and hold it, right from their phone.

**24.** No smartphone? No data? No problem. Dial a short code, and the confirmation arrives by SMS.

## Built to be trusted

**25.** Held beds are released automatically if the patient doesn't come, so no bed sits empty.

**26.** Old numbers are flagged, never hidden. And we share bed counts, never patient records.

## Where we are

**27.** The core system is already built and tested.

**28.** Next, we launch the apps for hospitals, dispatchers and families, connect real SMS and USSD, and pilot in our first district.

## Join us (finish strong; slow down on the last line)

**29.** We are looking for partner hospitals, the National Ambulance Service, and health leaders who believe this can change.

**30.** Together, we can end no bed syndrome.

**31.** Because no patient should ever die looking for a bed.
